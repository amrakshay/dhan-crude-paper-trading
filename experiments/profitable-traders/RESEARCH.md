# Who the profitable traders are, and how they make money

Compiled 2026-10-03 from two research passes, one on Indian evidence and one on
international evidence. Each claim was checked against the primary source where
it could be read. Anything taken only from press coverage is marked
**UNVERIFIED**, and figures marked *computed* are arithmetic on SEBI's tables.
Not investment advice.

**The limit of this document:** we can't see anyone's account. Everything here
comes from SEBI's published breakdowns of broker data, from SEBI's Jane Street
order, and from academic studies that had account-level data.

---

## 1. The short answer

1. **The money individuals lose mostly goes to institutions.** In FY26, prop
   firms made about +₹44,500 cr gross, FPIs +₹13,900 cr and corporates about
   +₹8,000 cr, while individuals lost −₹72,200 cr. **99%** of the prop and FPI
   profit came from algo entities, and **about 98%** of prop profit came from
   options. The top 10 prop firms took 74.5% of all prop profit. SEBI calls
   prop firms "the main liquidity providers" (they do about 60% of derivatives
   turnover). It publishes **no** split of how that profit is made: market
   making, arbitrage, option writing or direction.
2. **Among individuals, profit is extremely concentrated.** In FY25–26 about
   **0.2%** of individuals (about 26,200 people making more than ₹10 lakh)
   earned about **72%** of all individual profit (*computed*). Only 1.1% made
   more than ₹1 lakh.
3. **The individuals who win look different from the ones who lose:**
   - more capital;
   - less trading relative to that capital;
   - fewer trading days;
   - they are not pure option buyers;
   - costs take a smaller share of their result.

   These are associations. SEBI says its analysis is descriptive, so none of
   them is shown to be a cause.
4. **Skill that persists exists, but it is rare.** In Taiwan, fewer than 1% of
   day traders were *predictably* profitable. In India, 48% of those who made
   more than ₹10 lakh in FY22–24 were profitable again in FY25–26, against a 9%
   base rate. Yet only **0.5%** of the traders active in all five years
   (FY22–26) won in every one of them.
5. **The known sources of profit are:**
   - speed and scale (market making, HFT);
   - **selling volatility**, a risk premium that comes with crash risk;
   - in futures, being long in a rising market, which is beta rather than
     skill;
   - for a very small group, fast short-horizon prediction with aggressive
     orders.

   Discipline and low costs separate the winners from the losers, but they
   don't create an edge by themselves.

### 2a. The trader traits that go with winning (F&O, individuals)

| Trait | Loss-makers | Source |
|---|---|---|
| All individuals, FY26 | 87.7% (82.1% before costs) | P26 |
| **Only option buyers** (93% of traders) | **89.9%**; median return on peak margin **−114%** | B26 (sample of 5,050) |
| Mostly option buyers | 75.2%; median −15% | B26 |
| Mostly futures | 60.9%; median −13% | B26 |
| **Mostly option sellers** (2.1%, n ≈ 105) | **43.8%**; median **+1%**, the only positive median. But the average loss per losing seller is **₹51.7 lakh**, and sellers as a group were net negative (−₹543 cr). | B26 |
| Only futures (FY25–26) | 67.3%. Their P&L tracks NIFTY's direction (a long bias). | P26 |
| Peak margin < ₹1 L / ₹10 L–1 cr / > ₹1 cr | 90.0% / 67.6% / 36.4% (n = 22) | B26 |
| Equity portfolio < ₹1 L / > ₹1 cr / > ₹10 cr | 89.7% / 62.9% / 58% | P26 |
| Traded < 10 days / > 100 days | 83.3% / 89.4%. Those on > 100 days are 42% of traders and carry 87% of the losses. | B26 |
| Options turnover, low → high | about 86% → 94.7% | P26 |
| Turnover ₹10 L–1 cr on margin ₹10 k–1 L | **94–98%** | B26 |
| Age < 30 / > 60 | 88.6% / 81.0% | P26 |
| Women / men | 84.7% / 88.6% | P26 |
| Top-30 cities / the rest | 86.0% / 88.7%. Top-30 traders also churn far less: turnover is 29× their portfolio, against 72×. | P26 |
| Cash turnover > 5× their F&O turnover / < 10% of it | 84% / 90% | B26 |
| Years traded: 1 → 4 | 91.0% → 96.5%. **Experience doesn't help.** | B26 |

**Costs:** they take **18–24%** of winners' gross profit, and add **35–57%** on
top of losers' losses (P26, S24, ID24). In FY26, costs alone turned 4.4 lakh
gross winners into net losers.

**Persistence (B26):**
- Of those who lost in both of the two previous years, 90–92% lose again.
- Of the cohort that traded all five years, 0.5% won every year and 95.3% are
  net losers overall.
- Big past winners repeat at 35–48%, against a 9% base rate.
- Only 15.4% of trader-quarters are profitable.

### 2c. The one public look inside a professional book: Jane Street (JS)

- **Scale:** ₹36,502 cr of profit from 1 Jan 2023 to 31 Mar 2025. Index options
  made **+₹43,289 cr**, while stock futures lost ₹7,208 cr and cash lost
  ₹288 cr.
- **The pattern SEBI describes** (17 Jan 2024, +₹735 cr):
  - in the morning, buy ₹4,370 cr of Bank Nifty constituents and futures while
    building **7.3× that** in short delta through options;
  - after 11:49, dump the cash and futures legs.

  The cash and futures legs lost ₹61.6 cr. SEBI reads them as existing only to
  move the index in the options book's favour.
- **Outcome so far:** ₹4,843.57 cr was impounded and deposited, and the case is
  on appeal at SAT (adjourned Feb 2026; **final outcome UNVERIFIED**).
- **What it shows:** expiry-day index-option profit is taken from about 16
  lakh small directional option traders. It is **not** a method an individual
  could, or legally may, copy.

### 2d. The one Indian account-level academic study

**Agarwal, Ghosh, Prabhala & Zhao, "Animal Spirits on Steroids"** (2025). It
uses NSE account-level data for every retail option trade from 2007 to 2021
([PDF](https://www.fma.org/assets/docs/Derivatives2025/Zhao.pdf)).

- Day trading reached 90% of retail volume, and the median holding period is
  0 days.
- **Day-trading P&L is "flat to weakly positive". The large losses come from
  positions carried overnight**, which the authors read as a disposition
  effect.
- Positions through FinTech brokers lose about 3× as much.
- They find **no skill persistence at the monthly horizon**.
- Institutions are more often **net option sellers** with complex positions.
  Retail losses equal institutional gains in aggregate.

## 3. International evidence (account-level studies)

| Finding | Evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Fewer than 1% predictably profitable** | Taiwan, all day traders 1992–2006: about 13% profitable in a given year, but under 1% reliably so; 97% likely to lose going forward | Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, *J. Fin. Markets* 2014 ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v18y2014icp1-24.html)); *RAPS* 2020 |
| **Persistent winners exist** | The top 500 by past Sharpe earned **+37.9 bps/day net** the following year; the bottom earned −28.9 | BLLO 2014 |
| **Winners trade aggressively; they are not liquidity providers** | The top 500 used 71% aggressive orders, and their profits trace to those. Every passive-order decile lost. "Day traders are not profiting from providing liquidity." | BLLO 2011 WP ([PDF](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/day%20traders/Day%20Trading%20Skill%20110523.pdf)) |
| Liquidity provision pays **in aggregate**, but individuals don't keep it | Retail contrarian flow earns 19% a year at the portfolio level (40% in stress), yet individuals lose on the trade day. Limit orders get filled exactly when they are about to be wrong (adverse selection). | Barrot, Kaniel & Sraer, *JFE* 2016; Linnainmaa, *JF* 2010 |
| Brazil: no learning | Of 1,551 people who day-traded futures for more than 300 days, 97% lost and 1.1% earned more than the minimum wage | Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti 2019 ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101)) |
| **Selling volatility is the most successful style** | "For both retail and institutional investors" (KOSPI 200 account data). Long ATM straddles lose about 3% a week (the variance risk premium). | Hu, Kirilova, Park & Ryu, *Mgmt Sci* 2024; Coval & Shumway, *JF* 2001 |
| Retail option losses are concentrated in short-dated, near-the-money calls | Spread costs were 3.6× the trading loss. For 0DTE, more than 60% of losses are costs (**UNVERIFIED** detail). | Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya, *JF* 2023; Beckmeyer et al. 2023 |
| HFT profit comes from speed rank | Aggressive (liquidity-taking) HFTs earn the most. Average Sharpe is 9.2, and the profits persist. | Baron, Brogaard, Hagströmer & Kirilenko, *JFQA* 2019 |
| Discipline predicts survival | CME floor traders: relative trading discipline predicts later success. Prop traders take extra risk after a losing morning. | Locke & Mann, *JFE* 2005; Coval & Shumway, *JF* 2005 |
| Leverage caps cut losses, not edge | US retail FX leverage cap: −40% in losses for high-leverage traders. ESMA: 74–89% of retail CFD accounts lose. | Heimer & Simsek, *JFE* 2019; ESMA 2018 |
| "Experience helps" is mostly survivorship | The bad traders quit, and the ones who stay don't improve | Seru, Shumway & Stoffman, *RFS* 2010; Hayley & Marsh 2016 |

## 4. What separates winners from losers, ranked by evidence

| # | Pattern | Strength | Is it an **edge**, or hygiene? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Speed, scale and algorithms (prop firms, HFT, market making) | STRONG on *who*, WEAK on *how* | An edge, but **structural**: colocation, capital and queue priority. Not available to us. |
| 2 | Selling volatility rather than buying it | STRONG | A **risk premium** with crash exposure. Being paid to hold a tail, not skill. |
| 3 | Low turnover relative to capital, fewer trading days | STRONG | **Hygiene.** Losses fall, but it does not create profit. |
| 4 | Lower costs as a share of P&L | STRONG (partly mechanical) | Hygiene |
| 5 | Large capital or wealth | STRONG association | Unclear which way it runs: wealth may cause better outcomes, result from them, or reflect survivorship |
| 6 | Not a pure option buyer; futures longs | MODERATE | Futures gains are **beta** (long in up markets) |
| 7 | Fast, aggressive short-horizon prediction | MODERATE (Taiwan, under 1%) | A genuine skill edge, in a tiny and fast group |
| 8 | Avoiding lottery-like trades (short-dated near-the-money buying, crowded names) | MODERATE | Hygiene, plus not paying the premium |
| 9 | Discipline: no loss-chasing, cut losses | MODERATE | Hygiene |
| 10 | Experience | WEAK, mostly survivorship | — |

## 5. What this means for this repo

**Most of the "how" isn't available to a retail account:**
- #1 needs speed and scale.
- #2 is a premium for holding a tail risk.
- #6 is beta.
- #3, #4, #8 and #9 reduce losses rather than create profit.

That leaves very little that a retail account could genuinely copy as an edge.

**What we have already tested that maps onto this evidence:**

| Evidence | Our test | Result |
|---|---|---|
| #2, selling volatility | `hedged-option-selling`: the bull put spread on NIFTY | **+26% a year** on capital at risk over 2016–2026, but **−29% a year after SEBI's 2024-11-20 rules** (48 trades). The bear call spread was −88% a year, because of skew. |
| #7, short-horizon prediction | `intraday-screener` H1–H4: ORB, relative strength, VWAP pullback | Every one lost, even before costs |
| Liquidity provision / reversal | `intraday-screener` H3c gap fade | Direction information is real and survived the holdout, but it doesn't beat costs at 5 bps |
| Intraday shorts on bear days | `bear-intraday` | Fails. The fall happens before 09:30. |
| Day trading flat, overnight losses (Agarwal et al.) | — | **Not tested** |

**What the evidence suggests trying next, in order of how well-founded each
idea is.** None of these has been run yet.

1. **DONE 2026-10-03 → `../option-selling-post2024/README.md`.** The
   post-2024 loss is consistent with bad luck (p = 0.26). But the pre-2024 edge
   itself has t = 0.72, so it would take decades of trades to confirm.
   Recommendation: don't trade it, and don't forward-test it.
   *Original proposal:* **Volatility selling in the post-2024-11-20 market.** It is the
   strongest-evidenced retail-accessible style (Hu et al.; SEBI: sellers have
   the only positive median). It is also the only one that has worked on our
   data before. The open question is the post-break regime:
   - `hedged-option-selling` already used all the post-break data the cache
     holds (to 2026-09-17), and got −29% a year on only 48 monthly trades.
   - Re-running the same rule on the same data adds nothing.
   - The honest options are a **pre-registered forward paper-trading run**, or
     a **different, pre-registered cadence** (for example weekly entries, which
     gives about 4× the trades in the same window). Either must be judged on
     data the old result didn't see, or labelled as being on seen data.
2. **The lottery effect on NIFTY options**, measured rather than assumed.
   Compare the returns to **buying** OTM, ATM and ITM weeklies near expiry,
   net of costs, from the bhavcopy. If India repeats the US finding, the
   result is a map of which options retail overpays for. That tells the seller
   which strikes to sell. It is a descriptive study, not a strategy.
3. **Hygiene as rules for anything that does trade.** These cost nothing to
   adopt and every study agrees on them:
   - a turnover cap relative to capital;
   - a cap on trading days;
   - costs tracked as a share of P&L;
   - no adding after a loss;
   - no unhedged positions carried overnight.

   They belong in the paper-trading app's risk settings, not in a strategy.

**What I wouldn't do:** try to reproduce the prop/HFT edge (#1) or anything
resembling the Jane Street pattern. The first needs infrastructure no retail
account has. The second is the subject of a SEBI market-manipulation order.

## 6. Not knowable from public data

- How prop firms and FPIs make their money, by strategy. SEBI publishes none of
  it.
- Profit-makers split by expiry-day or 0DTE share, or by holding period.
- Buyer vs seller P&L across the whole population. The only cut is B26's
  5,050-trader sample.
- Whether the winning "individuals" are professionals in disguise (HNI desks,
  sub-brokers).
- Any causal effect of a trait.
