The left tail, first

This strategy family has a high win rate and a rare large loss. Every instinct built on breakout systems points the wrong way, so the tail is shown before the return and the win rate is greyed out.

Return, and what it was measured against

A market-neutral premium seller is not competing with the index. Its opportunity cost is the risk-free rate; its real comparator is the same strategy without the bought wing.

Equity and drawdown

Open positions are marked to market daily, so the drawdown is the one you would have lived through rather than what shows up at exits.

Margin utilisation

Margin is the binding constraint here, not cash — a credit structure receives money at entry and blocks margin against it. Peak utilisation is what decides whether a book survives a bad month, because the requirement expands exactly when the position is losing.

Per-trade P&L, with the tail shaded

The shape to look for is a tall stack of small wins and a thin arm reaching left. The mean of the worst 1% and 5% is marked.

Hedged against unhedged

The wing costs premium and releases margin. If the tail it removes is worth less than it costs, the honest answer is that this structure does not work at these prices — which is a finding, not a failure.

Backspreads — buy more wings than you sell

Sell one put at 16Δ, buy ratio puts at 5Δ below it. Below the long strike the extra wings outrun the short, so the loss peaks at the wing and then reverses: a crash stops being the disaster case. What is paid for it is a smaller credit every trade, and a lower median return.

Diagonals — sell the near expiry, buy further out in time

A two-expiry structure: the bought leg is further out of the money and longer dated, so it still holds time value when the short leg expires. Measured on a shorter window, because NIFTY did not carry four liquid expiries until weeklies matured.
